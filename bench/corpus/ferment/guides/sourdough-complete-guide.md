# Sourdough, the complete guide

Everything the S series taught me between September 2025 and July 2026, in one page. The house recipe lives in recipes/sourdough-country-loaf.md; individual bakes are logged as batch-S-xxx pages. This guide is the reasoning underneath all of that: what flour actually does, why temperature outranks every other variable I control, and how to read a loaf after the fact.

I bake in a small flat kitchen: no proofer, no stand mixer for most of the year, one oven with a hot left side, and a stone windowsill that could chill beer. If the numbers below work here, they survive almost anywhere.

A note on how to read this page: every claim that could be tested was tested on a numbered bake, and the log links are left in on purpose. Sourdough advice travels badly because it is usually written without its temperature context; the logs restore that context bake by bake. Where the guide and a log disagree, the log is newer and the guide is the summary — that ordering has been right so far, and when it stops being right, the correction goes here first.

## What actually matters, ranked

A year of logs points one way, and it is not the way I expected when I started:

1. Dough temperature, above all.
2. Fermentation end-point (the rise in the bulk container).
3. Flour character.
4. Hydration.
5. Shaping skill.
6. Everything else, including the starter's name and mythology.

Beginners — I was one, recently — obsess about 5 and 6. The loaves got dramatically better when I started obsessing about 1 and 2 instead. The rest of this guide is mostly those two ideas, unpacked.

## Flour

### Strong white: the chassis

The base of every bake here is a strong white bread flour. Protein content decides how much water the dough can carry and how much gas it can hold; mine sits around 12.5% protein, and that is the number I would look for anywhere. Below about 11.5%, loaves turn dense no matter what the schedule says, because the gluten web simply is not there to hold bubbles.

One habit worth stealing: buy flour in 5 kg bags and write the opening date on the side. White flour is at its best for about two months after milling, acceptable for six. The dull, flat-tasting loaves of S-010 and S-011 were both milled-flour-age problems I did not diagnose until S-019, and fixing nothing else, the loaves got sweeter.

### Dark rye: the flavour dial

10–20% dark rye is in every loaf here. It ferments fast, holds water strangely, and gives the crumb a toasted-seed sweetness with no seeds in sight. Above about 25%, structure suffers visibly; the crumb goes tight and faintly damp. Winter loaves here go to 20% rye deliberately — the starter loves it when the room is cold, and the flavour compensation is welcome when the crust pales.

Rye also started my starter (2025-09-20) and remains its favourite food. A single rye feed can rescue a jar that has gone flat on white alone; see guides/starter-rescue.md.

### Wholemeal: the colourful guest

Wholemeal brings bran, and bran is sharp little fragments that cut gluten strands while the dough works. The practical result: wholemeal loaves want a slightly gentler hand, slightly less water (the bran drinks plenty, but slowly), and they never achieve the open crumb of a white loaf. Mine go to 10% for flavour, and no further. The famous "wholemeal bakes denser" observation is real, and it is not your fault.

### Water absorption: how much can THIS bag take

The same recipe number printed on two different bags of flour can absorb wildly different water. The honest way to set hydration: mix at 70%, feel the dough after 20 minutes, and add the rest in dribbles until it turns from shaggy to silky. Mine usually drinks the full 72–76% of the country loaf, but a new bag gets the dribble treatment, because one 76% error taught me that a slack dough in a cold kitchen is how S-014 happened — dense, grey, humbling.

## Water, temperature, salt

- Water: tap water, rested an hour if the tap is fresh. I have never filtered anything for bread and never noticed a difference.
- Temperature: the lever nobody hands you at the start. Water temperature is the one input you can set exactly, so dough temperature becomes arithmetic: the flour, the room and the friction of mixing add up, and the water cancels the difference. Cold kitchen in December, dough wants 32 °C water. Hot week in July, dough gets water straight from the cold tap, sometimes with an ice cube per 500 ml.
- Salt: 2%, always weighed. Salt tightens gluten and slows fermentation slightly — both good, both worth the cost. Fine sea salt, non-iodised, nothing fancy.

## The levain

### Building it from the jar

The jar in the fridge is a reserve, not an ingredient. The night before a bake, take 30 g of it and build: 30 g starter : 60 g water : 60 g flour (the 1:2:2 you will see all over this wiki), on the warm shelf above the kettle. By morning it should be domed, jiggly and smell faintly of yoghurt and cream. That dome is the pass mark; anything flat or sharp-scented gets another feed before the bake proceeds.

### How ripe is ripe

Ripe levain has risen 50–100%, holds its dome, and floats reluctantly in water (the float test is real but sloppy; the dome is better). Under-ripe tastes of raw flour and rises weakly; over-ripe has flattened and turned sharp — you can bake with it, but the loaf will lean sour and the rise will be a compromise. The window at 24 °C is roughly 4–8 hours after feeding, which is why the overnight build at cooler shelf temperatures is so convenient: it stretches the window to about when the kettle boils for coffee.

### Ratios: 1:2:2, 1:4:4, and when

- 1:2:2 is the standard evening build and the rescue feed (guides/starter-rescue.md).
- 1:4:4 is the wake-up feed for a jar that has lived in the fridge for weeks: more food, longer runway, and by the second round it behaves like a normal jar again. S-021's evening feed was exactly this before its famous morning.
- Twice-a-day 1:2:2 for two days is the fix for a starving jar — the sharp-nosed state described in troubleshooting/starter-smell-triage.md.

## Mixing

### The autolyse

Flour and water alone, 1 hour, no salt, no levain. The flour hydrates, the gluten organises itself without any help, and the subsequent mix takes half the effort. In winter I stretch this to 90 minutes with the bowl near (not on) the radiator; in summer, 45 minutes is plenty. The autolyse is also the right moment to do the dribble test from the flour section — adjust water while the dough is still blameless.

### Incorporating levain and salt

Pinch and fold the levain through the autolysed dough for a minute, rest 10, then do the same with the salt. The dough changes from slack to elastic inside ten minutes of the salt going in. No stand mixer has ever been missed at this stage; hands are better instruments for feeling the moment the dough stops tearing and starts stretching.

### Machines and other shortcuts

I borrowed a stand mixer for two bakes (S-016, S-017). Verdict logged at the time: it saved 10 minutes and taught me nothing, and the loaves were indistinguishable from hand-mixed neighbours. If you have one, fine, but do not let anyone tell you the mixer is the difference between dense bread and open bread. That difference lives in the bulk.

### Stirring versus folding: the four-jar week

Early on I read that vigorous stirring in the first minutes builds more strength than folding later, so for one week (S-018 through S-021's neighbours) I split batches: half stirred hard for two minutes at mix, half folded as usual. The stirred doughs were visibly stronger at shaping; the loaves were close enough that I could not reliably tell them apart blind. Conclusion logged: stirring saves maybe one fold set, and the fold schedule is not the bottleneck in my kitchen. I still stir the first minute because it is satisfying, and I do not pretend it is load-bearing.

## Bulk fermentation

The bulk is where bread is actually made. Everything else is logistics.

### Dough temperature is the master dial

Target 24–26 °C dough temperature through the bulk. Every degree away from that changes the schedule by roughly 20–30 minutes; a 17 °C kitchen can turn a 4-hour bulk into an 8-hour one, which is exactly what happened across the December bakes (S-012 through S-014) before I believed it. The fix that unlocked winter: warm water (32 °C) to land the dough at 24 °C at mix, then the oven with ONLY the light on, door cracked, as a bulk container that holds about 24 °C. A strip thermometer on the bowl is the cheapest upgrade in this entire wiki.

### Stretch and folds

Four sets in the first two hours, roughly every 30 minutes: grab the dough from one side, stretch up, fold over, rotate, repeat around the bowl. That is the whole technique. It organises strength without knocking gas out, and by set three the dough holds its shape in the bowl instead of spreading like pancake batter. After the last set, hands off; the yeast has the floor.

### The 50% rule

End the bulk when the dough has risen about 50%, feels alive under the surface, and shows a few bubbles at the edges — NOT when it has doubled. Every dense, grey-crumbed loaf in the log was bulked too long or too warm; every gummy one was cut hot, a different crime entirely. The container-with-a-straight-side trick: mark the starting level with tape, judge 50% by eye against the tape, and stop trusting recipes that say "double". Flour weights, bowl shapes and temperatures differ; 50% at 25 °C is the transferable constant.

### Overruns and what they taste like

An over-bulked dough bakes into a loaf that spreads rather than springs, with a tight band of crumb just under the crust and a flat top. The flavour is fine, which is the insult: you do all that waiting and the reward is a frisbee. The recovery move, if you catch it in time, is straight to shaping and the fridge, skipping the rest of the schedule — the cold stops the clock. Twice, on S-015 and the August S-023, this save worked so well that the loaves were among the year's best.

## The grain experiments

### Spelt, one month of it

March 2026 was spelt month: four bakes at 25%, 40% and a reckless 60% whole spelt against white. Spelt gluten is genuinely different — quicker to strengthen, quicker to collapse — and the log reads like a cautionary tale. The 25% loaf was lovely, nutty and open; the 40% needed a gentler fold schedule and still came out half a centimetre flatter; the 60% loaf tasted wonderful and looked like a doorstop. Verdict, written on the S-020 page and repeated here: spelt stays at 25% and gets folded one set less, not more.

### Oats, which never quite belong

Rolled oats soak up water like gossip and give nothing structural back. The best oat loaf of the trials (10% soaked oats, 20 min in the autolyse water) was soft, faintly sweet and kept better than any other loaf that week — oats clearly belong in the soft, keeping-bread lane, not the open-crumb lane. Toasted oat topping, pressed on after shaping, is the version that earns its place permanently.

### What the experiments changed in the default

Nothing structural: the country loaf kept its 10–20% dark rye and white chassis. What they changed was respect for the 50% rule — every grain trial succeeded or failed on the bulk end-point, never on the grain itself. That is the quiet summary of this whole guide wearing a different hat.

## Feeding a culture without waste

The discard jar lives in the fridge door next to the miso crock and receives everything the ratio arithmetic trims away. It becomes, in rough rotation: pancake batter (discard, an egg, a pinch of sugar, fried in butter — the single best argument for maintaining a starter), crackers rolled paper-thin and salted hard, and the weekly crumb-coating for fried aubergine. Roughly 400 g of flour's worth of discard a month becomes food instead of guilt.

The arithmetic worth internalising: a jar maintained at 50 g with 1:2:2 feedings consumes about 1.5 kg of flour a month at daily feeding, and half that on an every-other-day rhythm. Feeding a big jar "so there is plenty" is how flour bills double for no gain — the levain section's 100 g build covers any bake here from a maintained 30–50 g jar.

## Shaping

### Preshape and bench rest

Tip the dough onto a lightly wetted counter, fold it into a rough round, and walk away for 20 minutes. The bench rest lets the outer skin re-form so the final shape can build real tension. Skip it and the final shape fights a sticky, torn surface; I skipped it exactly twice, and both loaves spread in the oven like they had given up.

### Building tension

Final shape on a barely-floured surface: flatten gently into a rectangle, fold the top third down, the bottom third up, roll away from you while dragging lightly to tension the skin, seal the seam. The goal is a taut drum, not a tidy cylinder. Into the floured banneton seam-up, pinch the seam, into the fridge. Sloppy shaping costs a little height; sloppy tension costs the whole loaf's posture.

## Scoring

### The blade

A razor blade on a coffee-stirrer handle (the classic lame, made for pennies) or the sharpest knife you own held at a shallow angle. Angle matters more than sharpness lore suggests: 30 degrees or so, slicing a flap rather than gouging a trench. The flap lifts as the loaf springs and becomes the ridge; a vertical cut just heals itself shut, which is why my first month of vertical slashes produced loaves that looked unscathed and burst out the side.

### Patterns and what they actually change

One decisive slash, slightly off-centre, is the workhorse: it directs the spring upward and the loaf opens where you chose. The fancy wheat-stencil patterns are real and I did them twice for guests; they cost spring because every cut is a weak point, and the loaf knows it. Practical scoring rules from the log: one cut for daily loaves, score deeper (1.5 cm) for loaves going straight from a long cold hold because the skin is tougher, and never score a slack, over-bulked dough anywhere but a single shallow line — it has nothing left to spend.

## Levain myths I retired

Written down so I stop re-litigating them at dinner parties:

- "The starter must be fed at exactly the same time daily." It is a culture, not a cat. Mine has survived bank holidays, a house-sitter and one memorable week of 1:4:4 neglect feeding, all logged, all recovered.
- "Metal spoils it." I stir with a steel spoon every day. The jars are glass, the spoons are steel, the bread is excellent. The myth probably descends from old reactive containers, not from metal itself.
- "You must discard or it dies." Discard is a schedule tool, not a life-support requirement. What actually matters is the ratio: feed past about 1:5:5 without discarding and the food is spread too thin to rise anyone. Discard is just how you keep ratios honest without owning a barrel of flour.
- "Rye starters are different species." Mine converts between rye-heavy and white-heavy feeding in a single cycle. Flavour shifts; identity does not.


## The cold hold

### Why overnight helps

The fridge does three jobs at once: it stops the yeast (the rise clock pauses), it keeps going the slower work of flavour and acid (which is where the sour-sweet complexity of a good loaf comes from), and it firms the dough so scoring and handling stop being fights. Twelve hours is the sweet spot here; 8 is a busy-day compromise; past 18 the crust pales and the acid gets shouty.

### My standard schedule

The paragraph every bake actually runs on, and the one I check before anything else: take the mixed dough to 26 °C through the bulk, shape by early evening, and have it in the fridge by 21:00. Bake straight from cold the next day: lid on at 230 °C for 20 minutes, lid off, 210 °C for 28 more, then 5 minutes out of the pot on the rack to set the base. That schedule has produced the most consistent crumb of the whole S series, and it survives my terrible oven because both temperatures have 15 minutes of slack in them before anything burns.

## Baking

### The covered phase

Dutch oven (or any heavy pot with a lid) preheated empty for 45 minutes at full temperature. Dough in on parchment, scored once, decisively, about a centimetre deep at a shallow angle. Lid on: the loaf steams itself, the crust stays rubbery and stretchy, and the spring happens. Twenty minutes covered, always, regardless of what the crust looks like — opening early is the classic way to lose the spring you spent a day earning.

### The open phase

Lid off, temperature down, crust to colour. Here the loaf finishes its story: dark mahogany at the blistered shoulder, pale gold on the sides. I pull it when the crust knocks hollow and the internal temperature reads 96–98 °C on the probe. Under that, the crumb stays gummy; much over, the crust goes from dark to bitter.

### Steam without a Dutch oven

A tray of preheated cast iron beneath the loaf, a cup of water thrown in at bake time, oven door closed fast. It works — S-018 was baked this way while the pot was on loan — but the crust is thinner and the spring is a notch down. The Dutch oven stays the default. Whoever invented the preheated-lid steam trick has my respect but not my cupboard space.

### Knowing when it is done

Probe temperature is the honest answer: 96–98 °C in the centre. Hollow knock is a folk test that works on the base, sometimes. Cool on a rack, and do not cut before one hour — the crumb is still setting and every premature slice is glue. This is the rule I break most often and regret most reliably; the S-019 photo of the steamed-open gash exists as a warning to me specifically.

## Reading the crumb

The loaf is a report card. What follows is the decoder ring, assembled one failure at a time.

### Tight, dense, even crumb with a grey cast

Under-fermented bulk, almost always tied to a cold kitchen. The December loaves (S-012, S-013, S-014) all wore this signature before the temperature work. The dough never built enough gas; no shaping or baking trick can put air into a loaf that never made any.

### Gummy, damp crumb near the centre

Under-baked, or cut hot. Probe to 96–98 °C, cool one hour. If the crust is already very dark and the centre still gummy, the oven ran too hot: drop the open phase by 10 degrees next time.

### Flat, wet-looking, spreading loaf with a tight band under the crust

Over-bulked. See the overrun section; the cold-save works if you catch it, and the 50% rule prevents it.

### Huge irregular holes under a pale, thick crust

Overnight hold went too long and the oven ran too cool. Keep the hold under 18 hours and trust the covered phase to do its job. Blistered shoulders are a prize; a pale crust with sparse blisters is the hold saying "I was down here a long time".

## Storage, staleness and second lives

The crust rule: never plastic. A loaf stored cut-side down on the board lasts a crust-worthy two days here; in a plastic bag the same loaf turns leathery overnight. Past day two, slices go to the freezer bag directly — toast test confirms the crumb survives (see the February fridge note), and frozen slices go straight into the toaster without thawing.

Stale bread is an ingredient queue, not waste: half a stale loaf becomes panzanella or breadcrumbs within the week; the heels dry in the oven's residual heat and end up as crumb for frying; the sad end-slice of every bake goes into the freezer for future breadcrumb emergencies. One dense December loaf (S-012) was too sad to eat fresh and became the best breadcrumbs of the year, which is the correct consolation for a failed crumb.

## Equipment, honestly reviewed

- Dutch oven: the single piece that changed the crust. Any heavy pot with a lid that survives 250 °C does the same job; mine is a no-name cast iron that lives on top of the fridge between bakes.
- Banneton: pretty spiral, useful shape, not magic — the first twenty bakes used a bowl and a tea towel with identical crumb.
- Strip thermometer on the mixing bowl: the cheapest, highest-leverage tool in the kitchen. It converted the bulk from folklore to arithmetic.
- Probe thermometer for the internal crumb check: ended the gummy-centre era permanently.
- Lame: a razor on a stirrer stick. The shop versions are nicer to hold and cost a hundred times more.
- Baking stone: borrowed, returned. The Dutch oven made it redundant for loaves; it now toasts flatbreads competently.
- The stone windowsill: a 14 °C corner of shame in winter and the best air-conditioned bulk spot in August. Every kitchen has one; find yours.

## A year of seasonal notes

### Winter (December–February)

Kitchen floor of 16–18 °C. The whole kit applies: warm-water mixes, light-on oven as a proofer, rye up to 20%, and the bulk is never under 6 hours. The winter loaves taste the deepest of the year, which is the consolation prize for the schedule owning your calendar.

### Summer (June–August)

The problem inverts: 27–29 °C rooms push the bulk to finish in under 3 hours. Cold water, ice cubes in the mixing water, the bulk on the stone windowsill (finally a use for it), and the fridge save on standby. Watch the dough, not the clock; the clock is a winter document.

### What the S series taught, in one line each

- S-014: dough temperature is not a detail; it is the game.
- S-018: you can bake without the Dutch oven, but not as well.
- S-019: the hour of cooling is part of the recipe.
- S-021: when everything else is right, the loaf is right — no heroics required.
- S-023: the cold save turns disasters into bread more often than not.

## Questions I actually get

**Is my starter dead?** Almost never. Sharp nose and grey liquid is hunger, and the rescue guide fixes it in two days. True death — pink or orange tint, putrid nose that survives two good feeds — I have never personally witnessed, only read about.

**Can I skip the autolyse?** Yes, and the dough will be slightly harder to handle and the crumb slightly tighter. On busy evenings I skip it without shame; the loaf forgives this specific corner more than any other.

**Why is my crust soft by evening?** It is the weather, not the bake — humid days soften crusts. Recrisp at 200 °C for 5 minutes and store the loaf cut-side down on the board, never in plastic.

**Whole rye instead of dark rye?** Works, darker and denser, slightly earthier. Keep it at 10% the first time; rye punishes greed.

**Do I need a banneton?** A bowl and a well-floured tea towel did the job for the first twenty bakes here. The banneton's spiral is pretty on the flour top; that is the whole upgrade.

**What is the single best upgrade?** The strip thermometer on the mixing bowl, then the probe thermometer for the crumb. Together they cost less than a bag of good flour and they replaced guessing with reading.

**Can the schedule compress into one day?** Yes, with a cost. A same-day loaf here looks like: morning levain build, noon mix, five-hour bulk at 25 °C, shape, two-hour counter proof in a cool spot, evening bake. The crumb is fine; the flavour is shallower, missing the cold hold's slow acids. I do it maybe four times a year and always notice.

**Is a dark crust just burnt crust?** No. The line between mahogany and bitter is wide, and loaves pulled at proper internal temperature with a deep crust taste sweeter, not harsher — crust colour is flavour that timid baking leaves on the table. My own loaves went paler than they should have for two months because I mistrusted colour; the probe ended that fear.

**Why do my blisters vanish?** Big shoulder blisters come from a cold skin hitting hot air: straight-from-fridge baking, a slightly drier surface, and patience with the covered phase. Room-temperature proofed loaves here rarely blister well. It is cosmetic, but it is a good tell that the cold hold did its job.

**Does the bread need a bread flour with additives?** Mine has none and does not miss them. The protein number does the work. Anything above roughly 12% protein with a short ingredient list bakes the same loaf in my kitchen.

**How do you keep the oven's hot left side from burning one shoulder?** Rotate the pot 180 degrees when the lid comes off. Ten seconds of work; every loaf since has been even. The oven keeps its opinion anyway, but now it expresses it symmetrically.

**Is sourdough actually better for you than shop bread?** I am a hobbyist with a notebook, not a nutritionist, so the honest answer from this kitchen is narrower: the long cold hold makes loaves that taste more and stale slower, and the fermentation clearly changes the flavour and keeping qualities in ways I can measure at the board. Whatever else the internet claims, that is as far as my logs reach. The J-shaped pattern of enthusiasm and evidence deserves the caution it gets, and the bread is good enough not to need exaggerating.
