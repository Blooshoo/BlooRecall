# Rescuing a run-down starter

Written 2026-06-13, collected from a year of S-series near-deaths. The rye starter has been through a cold snap, a three-week holiday neglect and one jar of dried-out scraping; all three came back, and here is how each was diagnosed.

## Reading the nose first

Before looking at rise or texture, open the jar and sniff. The nose tells me which state the jar is in; the look only confirms it. Everything below is keyed to that first sniff.

### When the starter smells like acetone

That sharp, paint-thinner character means hunger — the culture has eaten everything available and has started chewing on itself. The fix that has never failed: feed 1:2:2 morning and night (30 g starter : 60 g water : 60 g flour), keep the jar on the shelf above the kettle, and it rebalances within about two days. If the jar is also separating into layers, cut the water back by 10 g for one feed.

### When the nose goes dairy-gone-bad

A heavier, sweeter note, like milk left out too long, shows up when the jar sits warm and underfed at the same time. One feed with wholemeal flour, a cooler spot, and normal feedings after; mine settle within a day.

## Reading the look

### The dark layer of water on top

Hunger again, further along. Pour it off or stir it back in, then give 1:2:2 for two days before baking from the jar. The S-014 week in December 2025 was diagnosed exactly this way after the cold snap.

### Slow rise but a clean nose

Usually flour or temperature, not the culture. My kitchen at 17 °C stalls everything: warm the water to 30 °C before mixing the feed, and switch one feed a day to dark rye for three days. That combination has revived every sluggish winter jar since.

## The three-day rebuild

For a jar that can barely lift at all: day 1, 1:1:1 morning and evening; day 2, 1:2:2 twice; day 3, 1:2:3 in the morning, and it should double within 5 hours at 24 °C. That rhythm has recovered every jar I have bothered to save, including after the 2025 Christmas trip, when the poor thing spent three weeks in the fridge smelling of regret and still made bread by the following Tuesday.
