# A year of kombucha

Twelve months and fifty numbered cycles (the K series), October 2025 to October 2026, condensed into one page. Batch logs live in the logs folder; this is the reasoning and the constants underneath them. I brew for one household of two heavy tea drinkers, in a kitchen that swings from 16 °C in January to 29 °C in July, which turned out to be the entire education.

The short version up front, for the impatient: black tea, 50-ish grams of sugar per litre, 10% starter liquid, warm and dark for a week to ten days, bottle with fruit for two days, fridge, repeat weekly. Everything below is the footnotes to that sentence, and the footnotes are where a year went.

A second sentence for the skeptical: nothing in this page required anything fancier than jars, a mat, a strip thermometer and a kettle, and the two big lessons — temperature outranks recipe, and taste outranks calendar — were both learned expensively enough that writing them down felt like insurance.

## How the numbering works

Every cycle gets a K number, including the failures and the dumped batches — K-019's stingy starter experiment and K-023's oil-stalled flavoured tea keep their numbers like everyone else. The numbers also count split and restarted cycles, which is why the series moves faster than the calendar suggests: some summer weeks ran two small cycles, and one February week (the continuous-vessel month) produced three draws logged under three numbers. The rule: if liquid moved vessel and got tasted, it gets a number. That policy is why "the sharpest batch" and "the best batch" can both be found by number later, which is the entire point of a log.

## The setup

### Vessels

Three vessels in service across the year. A 2.5 L glass jar (the workhorse, September 2025 to January 2026), a 3.2 L wide jar (February onward, wider surface means faster cycles at the same room temperature), and the 3.8 L clip-lid jar that turned out to be the best of the three for summer brewing because its lid, resting but not clipped, let it ride heatwaves without souring overnight. Bigger vessels did not make more kombucha so much as they made the schedule forgiving; a 1 L jar punishes a late harvest day, the 3.2 L shrugs.

### Covers and light

Cloth squares held with elastic, doubled in summer against fruit flies (they found the jar in July 2025 within 48 hours; the doubled cloth ended their season). Light: the jars live in the shadowed corner of the counter because direct sun warmed them unevenly and bleached the culture pale. The sticker strip thermometer on the front glass reads the vessel, not the room, which settled more arguments with myself than any other single piece of equipment here.

### Where the jars live through the year

October to April: on the seedling heat mat at 26 °C, because the kitchen floor sits at 16–18 °C and the culture sulks below 20. May and September: ambient on the counter. June to August: the coolest shelf, out of sun, sometimes with a damp cloth over the glass for evaporative cooling during the late-July heatwave. The year-round summary is that location is the recipe's hidden ingredient; the same vessel, tea and sugar produce visibly different drinks from different corners of one small kitchen.

## Tea

### Black, the base

Cheap plain black tea bags, four to the 2.5 L vessel, five to the 3 L equivalent. Every one of the fifty cycles used it as the backbone; the culture visibly prefers it, forming a new pancake layer in days, where green-blended cycles took a week and a half. The T-series of tea trials (logged informally in K-018 through K-024) settled the house standard: black base, no perfume, no oils.

### Green and the hybrid months

Pure green-tea cycles (three of them, spring) made a lighter, almost honeyed drink that my flatmate preferred and I found thin. The compromise that stuck for spring batches: three black bags plus one green. The culture stayed vigorous, the drink kept some of the green sweetness. Noted for next spring, along with the cost quirk: decent green tea costs three times the black, and the difference in the glass was not three times better.

### The flavoured-tea failures

Two cycles with a shop "forest fruits" black tea (K-022, K-023) both stalled: the fruit-flavouring oils sat in a film on the surface and the new culture layer came in patchy and grey. Both batches tasted fine after a normal cycle length, but the risk-to-benefit was all risk. Rule written into the house standard the same week: plain teas only in the vessel; all flavour belongs to the second stage.

## Sugar

### White, and only white

Plain white sugar, every cycle, roughly 50 g per litre brewed (45 in the 2.5 L winter vessel, 55–60 when the mat runs hot in K-041). Unrefined and brown sugars were tried twice (K-014, K-027): both left a molasses heaviness and, more tellingly, the culture layer came in thin. The minerals that make unrefined sugar interesting to humans apparently do nothing for the culture. White sugar is cheaper anyway; the fancy-sugar experiment was retired without ceremony.

### How much per litre, and where it ends up

45–60 g per litre in the first stage leaves a finished drink that is dry but not vinegary at day 8–10. The sugar is not in the glass by then — the culture eats nearly all of it, which is the whole point — and the sweetness in a finished bottle comes almost entirely from the second stage's fruit and its own small sugar top-up. Understanding that division of labour was the month-ten clarity: stage one is for dryness and structure, stage two is for flavour.

## The brewing cycle

### Starter liquid, the 10% rule

Ten percent of the new batch's volume comes from the old batch as starter liquid, plus the pancake itself. Under 10% and cycles stretched visibly — the one 5% experiment (K-019, out of stinginess during a batch that went to vinegar) took thirteen days. Over 20% and the cycle sours faster than flavour develops; K-020's over-inoculated batch was drinkable at day 5 and past it by day 8. Ten percent is not tradition; it is the middle of the measured channel.

### Handling the pancake

Clean hands, no metal beyond the inevitable spoon once in a while (no harm was ever traceable to steel here, same finding as the sourdough spoon). The pancake can be rinsed in a little finished kombucha if it gets stringy; the brown strings hanging underneath are normal and strain out at bottling. It thickens in cold months and thins in hot ones; a thin culture with fast cycles is healthy, not dying — my August panic about exactly this was settled by tasting the batch, which was perfect.

### When to harvest

Day 7–10 at 24 °C; the strip on the glass and the calendar both matter, and the tongue decides. The harvest tells in order: the surface smell turns from bready to bright, the sweetness drops under the acidity instead of over it, and a fresh culture layer has formed on top. Harvesting on taste rather than day count is the difference between the sharp-cider accidents of autumn 2025 and the consistent run since February.

### The weekly rhythm, Sunday to Sunday

The settled house schedule, for the record: Sunday morning, brew the sweet tea (kettle, bags, sugar stirred in cold enough not to shock anything); Sunday evening, everything into the vessel with starter liquid and pancake. The vessel sits untouched Monday to Saturday with one smell check from Wednesday. Saturday or Sunday, harvest, bottle with fruit, and the bottles ride their own two-day clock while the next sweet tea cools. Ten minutes of active work on each end, one hour of washing between. The rhythm survived holidays by shifting a day in either direction without visible harm — the culture is more forgiving of a late harvest than an early one, which is the single most useful asymmetry in this whole hobby.

## Cleaning, and the one vinegar accident

The washing routine is boring on purpose: hot water and a dedicated bottle brush, no soap in the brewing vessel (a soap-filmmed vessel in November cost K-013 its vigour for a cycle; a hot-water rinse restored it). The accident of the year was K-044, the cycle that went on holiday with me for sixteen days and came back as genuine vinegar — sharp, clean, actually useful. It became the starter liquid for the V-series vinegar crock experiments and the reason the V-011 red wine batch has a culture cousin. The lesson was not how to prevent it (a neighbour watering plants could have harvested; the culture did what long time and warm rooms always do) but what to do after: vinegar is not a failure, it is a fork in the road with its own label.

## Temperature through the seasons

This deserves its own section because it reordered the whole operation. At 26 °C (heat mat, winter), cycles run 7–8 days and the drink lands dry and rounded. At 20 °C (shoulder seasons), 9–11 days, softer acidity, my personal favourite of the year. At 29 °C (the July heatwave, K-046, K-047), the cycle wanted finishing at day 5–6 or it tipped into sharpness by day 8. Nothing else — not tea, not sugar, not vessel — moved the calendar as much as the room did. The heating mat earns its corner from October to April; in summer, the problem inverts and the coolest shelf plus shorter cycles keep the drink in the channel.

### The three thermometers problem

The wall thermometer, the strip on the glass, and the cheap probe in the liquid disagreed all winter — the wall said 21, the strip said 24.5, the probe said 24. The wall was measuring the room's average, not the mat-warmed pocket where the jar sat. Trust order, settled in February: probe in the liquid for truth, strip on the glass for the daily glance, wall for deciding whether to open a window. Arguing with instruments is cheaper than arguing with a culture, but only if you know which instrument you are arguing with.

## Second stage and flavouring

### Fruit amounts

Two to three thin slices of fruit per 750 ml bottle is the calibrated house amount, settled across roughly thirty flavoured bottles. Ginger: two coins, bruised. Citrus: a twist of peel only, never segments (segment batches went bitter at day 3). Berries: four or five, split. Stone fruit, the summer triumph (K-049's peaches): three thin slices, 46 hours. The full December fruit error and its consequences are catalogued in the pomegranate incident log — the short version is that fruit plus extra sugar plus a warm radiator plus five forgotten days makes a bottle that opens like a geyser, and the lidded tub exists because of it.

### Herbs and the tea-bag trick

A mint sprig or two, 24 hours only (longer extracts a chlorophyll bitterness — the 48-hour mint batch tasted of lawn). Dried herbs in a clean tea ball: chamomile twice, both gentle and good. The trick worth keeping: after straining fruit out at refrigeration time, dropping a fresh bag of the SAME tea into the bottle for 30 minutes restores an aroma the second stage stripped. Small thing, big effect on how "brewed" the final glass tastes.

### Sweetness at the second stage

Half a teaspoon of sugar per 750 ml bottle, added ONLY when the first stage ran long and dry (past day 10). Most of the year the fruit alone carries it. The teaspoon-sugar habit from before February produced three over-carbonated batches and was retired to the "only if dry" clause it should always have been.

## Bottling

Twelve 750 ml flip-tops, two of which (gaskets hardened, failed the monthly water test in August) are on storage duty — see troubleshooting/kombucha-no-tingle.md for the still-bottle forensics. Every bottle sits its second stage inside the lidded plastic tub, because of the pomegranate incident, and the tub has already caught one leaker (a gasket-fail bottle in June would have syruped the counter). Bottles go to the fridge at the taste peak, usually 46–48 hours in summer, 60–72 in the cooler months. Chilling is not optional housekeeping; it is the brake, and the brake is part of the recipe.

## The flavour calendar of the year

What actually got bottled, month by month, which doubles as a shopping list from the future:

- **October–November 2025:** lemon-ginger only, because it was the only combination the first cycles could carry without embarrassment. Ginger coins plus a lemon twist, 60 hours.
- **December 2025:** the pomegranate month, ended by the pomegranate incident. Apple took over for the rest of the winter: two thin slices, reliable, quiet.
- **January–February 2026:** mulling-spice bottles (a cinnamon stick fragment, two cloves, 24 hours exactly) drank warm-ish from the fridge and divided the household. Logged as "winter is allowed one spice batch per month".
- **March–April:** the berry window opens. Four or five split strawberries per bottle, 48 hours, and the run of K-038 through K-040 was the best stretch of the spring.
- **May–June:** rhubarb, discovered late and hoarded after: one thin stick's worth of slices, 50 hours, needs the half-teaspoon sugar clause because rhubarb brings no sugar of its own.
- **July–August:** stone fruit season, the year's peak. Peaches (K-049), then apricots, all at 46 hours in the warm kitchen. Mint appeared twice as a garnish-length infusion and both times at exactly 24 hours, because 48 tastes of lawn.
- **September:** the grape fortnight — halved table grapes, surprisingly excellent, then back to apple as the market stalls turn over.

## Sharing the culture

Four pancakes' worth of culture left this kitchen in the year: two to friends with jars of finished liquid, one to the office fridge experiment (which died of neglect in a heatwave, logged by proxy), one into the compost after a month in the back of the fridge proved that a culture can wait but not indefinitely. The etiquette that worked: give culture and starter liquid together, in a jar with headspace, with a cloth taped over the lid for the journey, and write the feeding numbers on masking tape — the one-line version is 10% starter, plain black tea, 50 g of sugar per litre, a week at room temperature. Every recipient who got the numbers on tape is still brewing; the one who got a verbal explanation is not. Tape.

## The numbers I brew to

The constants I actually measure against, which took the year to stop changing — and the load-bearing one, buried here because it took ten months to earn its place: for the 3 L wide vessel, brew with 3 litres of water, 150 g of white sugar, five plain black tea bags, and keep back 300 ml of finished liquid as starter (the 10% rule, no rounding down), running the cycle 8 days at a steady 24 °C. That set of numbers has produced the house-standard glass every single time it was used; every deviation I tried moved at least one property the wrong way.

Around it, the quick constants: second stage 46–72 hours at room temperature then fridge; fruit two or three thin slices per 750 ml; harvest-smell test before any calendar trust; 10% starter, never less; plain tea only; white sugar only. Tape them inside the cupboard door; I did.

## Reading a batch that looks wrong but is not

- Brown strings drifting beneath the culture: normal, strain at bottling. A whole year of batches had them; none were ever a problem.
- A pale, thin culture layer in summer: normal. Fast cycles make thin pancakes; taste the liquid, which is the product.
- Holes and bumps in the pancake: normal texture, not damage.
- The liquid darkening over months: the culture builds pigment as it ages; a wash-and-reset (reserve starter, rinse vessel, rebuild) clears it, K-036's batch was the darkest and the reset worked first try.
- Cloudy liquid late in a long cycle: the culture shedding into the drink; fine at harvest, clearest if you let the vessel sit undisturbed an hour before drawing off.

What genuinely is wrong: fuzzy coloured mounds (see guides/surface-fuzz-and-film.md and its compost verdicts), oil films from flavoured teas, and a smell of actual rot instead of clean sharpness. One of those three happened twice in fifty cycles; both batches went out with the recycling and the vessel got scalded.

## Batch versus continuous

The continuous-vessel experiment ran one month (February, K-031 through K-035): draw off bottles, top up with fresh sweet tea, never fully harvest. The spigot clogged with strings twice, the flavour drifted sharper week on week, and the one deep-clean the vessel needed reset the whole culture. Batch brewing costs ten more minutes a week and buys back consistency; the continuous vessel is a good idea that loses to the dishwashing reality of a small kitchen. Verdict logged in K-035 and never revisited: batch, always, here.

## What it costs

Roughly: 5 bags of black tea and 150 g of sugar per 3 L cycle, call it well under the price of two shop bottles of kombucha, and the cycle fills five 750 ml bottles. The startup cost was the jars and the mat; everything else is pantry. The economics were never the point — the control was: this way the drink is exactly as dry, exactly as sour, and exactly the fruit I want, and there is always a bottle in the fridge.

## What I stopped doing

- Flavoured teas in the vessel (oils stall the culture layer).
- Unrefined sugar (molasses heaviness, thin culture).
- Extra sugar per bottle by default (only for dry, long first stages).
- Continuous vessel (spigot, drift, cleanup).
- Trusting the calendar over the smell test (autumn 2025's sharp-cider fortnight was calendar trust).
- Panic over thin summer cultures (taste the batch; the culture knows the season better than I do).

The year in one sentence: the culture is a houseplant that makes a drink — give it warmth, food and time, keep the calendar humble, and it repays you weekly.
