# Surface fuzz and film — a field guide

Written 2025-10-05, after the first scary week of the September kimchi. Every jar I have ever grown has grown something on top. Most of it is harmless, some of it is the culture itself, and a small set of things means the whole batch goes to the compost. This page is how I decide quickly, without a lab and without panic.

## The palette of normal

In a healthy brine ferment these are the only colours I expect at the top: creamy white, off-white, pale tan, and a grey-beige slick like wet paper. In kombucha the culture itself is beige to amber, smooth on top, with darker strings hanging underneath. Anything outside that palette earns suspicion, and anything fuzzy earns immediate attention.

## Smooth or fuzzy: the one test that matters

- Smooth, moist, flat or gently wrinkled: leave it alone or skim it. This is the aerobic crowd moving in — harmless, though a wrinkled top sheet eats aroma, so I lift it off when it forms.
- Dry, raised, fuzzy or hairy, growing in little mounds with coloured centres: the batch is finished. Not skimmable, not negotiable. The invisible part runs down into the liquid like roots, so scooping away the visible bit accomplishes nothing.

## What gets composted, jar and all

- Fuzzy mounds in blue, green, black or pink.
- Any fuzzy patch, any colour, sitting above the brine line on the shoulders — those shoulders stay contaminated even after scrubbing, so the jar goes to recycling along with the batch.
- Anything that smells of actual rot rather than clean sourness.

## The wrinkled blanket

When a tan sheet forms that wrinkles when poked, I skim it, wipe the rim, taste the ferment underneath and carry on. It shows up most in winter, on batches at the low end of the salt range, and on jars where the weights slipped. It is annoying, not dangerous; skimming daily for a week usually outlasts it.

## Kombucha specifics

The leathery pancake is the culture, not a problem; holes, bumps and pale patches on it are normal. Dry papery edges mean too much air is getting past the cloth — tighten it or top the liquid up. Brown strings hanging beneath the pancake are normal too; strain them at bottling and forget about them.

## Prevention, which is the whole game

Keep everything under the brine with real weights. Keep salt at 2% or above for vegetable work. Wipe rims weekly. Taste with a clean spoon, never double-dip, and keep lids and cloths as clean as plates. In four years of jars, every batch I lost traced back to a vegetable above the water line or a lazy lid.
