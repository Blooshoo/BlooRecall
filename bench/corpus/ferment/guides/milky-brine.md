# The milky look in the jar

Written 2025-11-15, the page I wish had existed on day three of my first sauerkraut.

By the second or third day, the liquid in my sauerkraut and pickles turns from glass-clear to milky, like a splash of milk stirred into water. The first time I saw it I nearly poured the batch down the sink. It turned out to be the single most reassuring thing the jar could tell me.

## Why it turns milky

The same bacteria doing the souring shed cell material, and they pull starches and proteins out of the vegetables as they work; the liquid carries all of it. Garlic-heavy jars go the milkiest — my November 2025 garlic-dill cucumbers looked like thin paint by day four. By day seven the jar often settles into layers, milkier above, clearer below. Both layers are fine.

## The checks I run

- Nose: clean and sour, yoghurt-adjacent. That is a working jar.
- Look: milky is right. Grey-black sludge, or a smell like a drain, is a bin — no tasting, no rescuing.
- Time: milkiness that persists for weeks in the fridge is not decay; it is the broth the ferment made. My day-40 kraut pours milky-gold and eats beautifully.

## Not to be confused with

- White dust rings on the glass above the water line: minerals left by the salt as it dries. Wipe off, ignore.
- White sediment at the bottom of the jar: normal, strains out at serving.
- A smooth tan sheet forming on top: that is a different guest entirely — skim it, see guides/surface-fuzz-and-film.md.

## The habit worth keeping

Photograph the jar on day one, day three and day seven. When day three looks alarming, the day-one photo proves it used to be clear and the change is just the work happening. I still do this with every new vegetable, and it has talked me out of two premature funerals.
