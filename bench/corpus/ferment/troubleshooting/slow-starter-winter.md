# Slow starter in winter

2025-12-14, written from the middle of the cold snap that produced the dense December loaves (see batch-S-014.md for the loaf-shaped evidence). Between November and March the kitchen floor sits at 16–18 °C, and the starter behaves like everything moved to half speed, because it did.

## The numbers

A jar that doubles in 5 hours at 24 °C takes 11–14 hours at 17 °C. Nothing is broken; the arithmetic of cold is just different. Once I measured this across three winter weeks, the mystery disappeared and the schedule became arithmetic instead of anxiety.

## What works here

- Warm the feed water to 30 °C in winter (mixing it cold in December was the S-014 mistake).
- The warm shelf above the kettle is the best real estate in the flat: a steady 22–24 °C day and night.
- The oven with ONLY the light on holds about 24 °C and makes an excellent overnight proofing box; a taped note on the dial has so far prevented the light becoming an oven.
- Shift one daily feed to dark rye during the coldest weeks — the culture visibly prefers it when cold and the rise comes back a notch.

## What does not work

- Bigger feedings to "give it strength": more flour just sits there at 17 °C.
- Moving the jar to the radiator side: surface heat cooks one side and the jar dries out; the shelf above, not the radiator itself.
- Declaring the starter dead: it has never been dead, it has been cold. The difference is smell and patience (troubleshooting/starter-smell-triage.md).

## The winter schedule that finally held

Evening: 1:2:2 feed with warm water on the kettle shelf. Morning: doubled and domed, build the levain straight from it. The December bakes after this change (S-015 onward) stopped being grey, and by S-021 the whole temperature doctrine graduated into guides/sourdough-complete-guide.md, which is where the general case lives; this page is the winter chapter of it.
